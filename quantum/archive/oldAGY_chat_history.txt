  Use Case categorisation & engineering for reuse
  
  Many Defence use cases can be expressed as combinations of a small number of recurring Computational Kernel Families (CKFs), such as constrained combinatorial optimisation, graph inference, statistical
  inference, or sequential decision-making under uncertainty. CKFs provide a compute-agnostic abstraction over specific models, algorithms, solvers and hardware technologies.
  
  Individual use cases are rarely associated with a single CKF. Instead, they are typically composed of different blends of multiple CKFs.  A useful analogy is to think of CKFs as the primary colours of
  computational problem solving.  As Defence develops experience across a larger body of use cases, patterns may emerge in how CKFs are repeatedly combined, sequenced and weighted across operational and strategic
  challenges.
  
  Some of these recurring patterns may ultimately crystallise into Operational Problem Types (OPTs). If CKFs are the primary colours, OPTs can be viewed as recurring colour palettes that emerge from repeated
  patterns across Defence challenges.
  
  This perspective shifts focus away from individual technologies and towards the enduring computational structures that underpin Defence challenges. It enables Defence to build reusable algorithmic competence,
  become a more informed buyer of emerging compute capabilities and more rapidly exploit algorithmic techniques to gain computational advantage.
  
  For a given use case, the important question is often not whether a particular technology is relevant, but which CKFs are constraining performance and therefore where advances in computation are most likely to
  provide value.   Understanding the relative contribution of different CKFs can help identify dominant bottlenecks, prioritise investment, and determine where advances in computation are most likely to deliver
  operational advantage.
  
  For example, it may be less useful to conclude that "Technology X benefits Use Case Y" and more useful to understand that "Use Case Y is primarily constrained by Graph Inference and only secondarily by
  Sequential Decision-Making. Improvements in the former are therefore more likely to deliver operational advantage."
  
  Over time, analysis of many use cases may reveal recurring patterns such as:
  Common combinations of CKFs.
  Typical workflows or dependencies between CKFs.
  Reusable approaches to solving common classes of Defence challenges.
  Areas where investment in a particular computational capability may deliver value across multiple use cases.
  
  These recurring patterns may ultimately lead to valuable Operational Problem Types, providing Defence with a reusable way to understand, compare and exploit future challenges. They may also accelerate the
  assessment of new use cases by identifying similarities with previously analysed challenges and their associated Computational Kernel Families. Finally, CKFs enable deeper technical assessment and tracking of
  changes in the relative benefits of different compute paradigms to occur independently and in parallel with the assessment of new demand and its mapping to potential solutions.
  
  Consideration of Use Cases beyond Optimisation
  Many benchmark libraries, algorithm communities and emerging-compute offerings are centred on optimisation problems. QOBLIB is a useful example, providing a common vocabulary and set of benchmark problem classes
  spanning areas such as assignment, scheduling, routing, packing and resource allocation. These benchmarks can help identify where a Defence use case relates to a well-understood optimisation family and where
  mature classical, hybrid or emerging computational approaches already exist.
  
  However, optimisation represents only one part of the broader computational landscape. Many Defence challenges are concerned with graph inference, statistical inference, simulation, quantum dynamics or
  cryptanalytic computation rather than optimisation. Consequently, not every use case will map cleanly to a QOBLIB problem class.
  
  A QOBLIB mapping is included where a use case contains a significant optimisation component, providing a bridge to established benchmark suites and performance comparisons.  The absence of a meaningful QOBLIB
  mapping is a useful indicator that the use case is dominated by a different Computational Kernel Family and should be analysed through an alternative computational lens.
